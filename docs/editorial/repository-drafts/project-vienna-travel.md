I now make a travel website for all my adventures and reference it during the trip. That is one of the clearest changes in my routine since I began working with coding agents in December 2025.

A trip produces information in several shapes: places on a map, a sequence of days, a packing list, and details about individual activities. Those pieces become more useful when I can navigate between them while traveling.

The goal is to give the trip a home that works as a reference during the adventure. For the Austria project, that means presenting the itinerary alongside the geographic and practical information needed to follow it.

[Austria Expedition](https://github.com/aranlucas/vienna-travel) is a Next.js itinerary with maps, GPX hikes, weather, packing, booking-related information, and a timeline. The structure makes the trip itself the organizing unit.

The public repository is deliberately a shareable version of that idea. Its documentation separates itinerary fixtures from private traveler details and booking references. A travel site can communicate the plan without making every piece of trip administration part of the public source.

What interests me about this format is the fit between the tool and the task. A coding agent can help assemble a dedicated interface around one adventure. The result can be small and specific because it only has to serve that trip.

Travel websites have become a recurring habit for me, and I consult them while traveling. That is a stronger result than simply having deployed another project: the artifact has a place in the activity it was made for.

This Austria repository is one example of the format. The next detail to capture is a moment from a particular trip when the site helped me choose a route, find a detail, or adjust the day. That will make the record more useful than a list of features.
